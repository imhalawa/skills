# Comparative analysis of a balanced technical-writing corpus

**Phase:** 2 — comparative artifact analysis

**Corpus frozen from:** [Technical-writing source census](technical-writing-source-census.md)

**Analysis date:** 2026-07-31

**Access date:** 2026-07-31 unless an artifact log entry says otherwise

## Research question and method

This study asks which explanatory practices recur across a purposive, genre-balanced sample of public, first-party technical communication, which practices conflict, and which are better treated as genre-specific options than universal rules. The unit of analysis is an artifact or a named, bounded section of a longer work. Sources are first compared within their census stratum; only then are observations synthesized across strata.

The census's proposed 18 sources were fixed before artifact reading. The target was two units per source. Same-stratum substitutions were permitted only for an inaccessible or unsuitable artifact or source, and all substitutions had to be logged. Required source families—Martin Fowler, ByteByteGo, High Scalability, Hello Interview, and official *Grokking Algorithms* material—had to remain represented.

The prior `common-patterns-great-technical-writing.md` report was deliberately not consulted or reused. This analysis was derived from the census, the frozen codebook below, and direct artifact inspection.

### Pre-reading codebook

The following codebook was fixed after reading the census and before reading the sampled artifacts. It defines what counts as evidence and prevents attractive examples from silently redefining the questions.

#### Evidence status

- **F — source fact:** directly stated metadata or content, such as a byline, date, declared audience, promise, rule, requirement, or limitation.
- **G — explicit guidance:** the author explicitly recommends or rejects a communication practice. This is available mainly in normative and pedagogical artifacts and is not treated as proof that all authors use the practice.
- **O — artifact observation:** an analyst-coded feature visible in the artifact, such as placing a worked example before a definition. It describes the artifact, not the author's intent or the feature's effectiveness.
- **I — inference:** a comparison or proposed explanation drawn across observations. Inferences are stated cautiously and never presented as source claims or causal findings.

For the evidence matrix, `G` means explicit author guidance, `O` means analyst observation, `G/O` means both occur in the source's sampled units, `—` means the candidate pattern was not observed in the sampled units, and `N/A` means the pattern is not meaningfully testable in that genre or available rendering. A mark is evidence of presence, not a quality score.

#### Coding dimensions

1. **Reader and audience contract (`AUD`)**
   - Record whether the audience, prerequisites, task, promised outcome, scope, and exclusions are explicit or inferable.
   - Code novice/intermediate/expert orientation and whether the artifact addresses a learner, practitioner, decision-maker, operator, or contributor.
2. **Opening move and load-bearing point (`OPEN`)**
   - Classify the opening as problem/pressure, outcome/promise, definition, concrete scene, question, anecdote, roadmap, or rule.
   - Record where the load-bearing claim or governing problem appears: title/lede, first section, middle, or conclusion.
3. **Concrete-to-abstract sequencing (`SEQ`)**
   - Code concrete→abstract, abstract→concrete, alternating/spiral, or reference-first sequencing.
   - Note progressive disclosure, running examples, revisiting at higher fidelity, and whether abstraction precedes a reason to care.
4. **Terminology introduction and consistency (`TERM`)**
   - Record whether terms are defined before, at, or after first use; whether plain language or an example precedes the label; and whether notation and synonyms remain stable.
   - Note metaphors, contrastive definitions, glossaries, and any acknowledged overloaded terms.
5. **Example type and technical function (`EX`)**
   - Classify examples as toy, realistic case, production case, trace, analogy, counterexample, failure, or exercise input.
   - Code their function: motivate, instantiate, reveal mechanism, visualize state, test a rule, expose a boundary, support transfer, or validate an outcome.
6. **Diagrams, interaction, code, and formalism (`MOD`)**
   - Record the modalities used and whether each is decorative, redundant, complementary, or load-bearing.
   - Note proximity to prose, captions/labels, reader control, animation/state, runnable versus illustrative code, and the timing of equations or notation.
7. **Causal explanation and state change (`CAUSE`)**
   - Look for mechanism chains, before/after states, transitions, invariants, dependencies, and explicit links from action or condition to consequence.
   - Distinguish descriptions of components from explanations of why behavior occurs.
8. **Tradeoffs, guarantees, edge cases, and failure modes (`RISK`)**
   - Record explicit constraints, alternatives, costs, guarantees/invariants, counterexamples, operational failures, edge cases, and recovery or mitigation.
   - Note whether qualifications appear beside the main claim or only later.
9. **Sentence/paragraph structure and tone (`PROSE`)**
   - Code topic-led versus narrative paragraphs, paragraph focus, sentence complexity, active/direct constructions, second person, questions, imperatives, hedging, humor, analogy, and institutional versus personal voice.
   - These are qualitative observations; no readability or sentence-length metric is inferred without measurement.
10. **Navigation and scannability (`NAV`)**
    - Record descriptive headings, tables of contents, summaries, numbered procedures, callouts, captions, cross-links, quick references, and layered overview/deep-dive paths.
    - Distinguish navigation for linear reading from navigation for lookup or task completion.
11. **Verification, exercises, and expected outcomes (`VERIFY`)**
    - Record runnable checks, expected output, exercises, quizzes, answer keys, manipulable inputs, comprehension prompts, tests, or observable success criteria.
    - Distinguish learner practice from evidence supporting the artifact's own technical claim.
12. **Genre and medium constraints (`GENRE`)**
    - Record genre, length/scope, editorial setting, date/update status, dynamic behavior, paywall/login limits, transcript quality, and provenance.
    - Interpret every other code in light of the artifact's job: essay, visual explainer, architecture profile, tutorial/book chapter, course, or normative reference.

### Decision rules

- A **recurring pattern** must appear in sampled units from at least three strata and at least six sources; otherwise it is reported as a local tendency or illustrative case.
- A **meaningful disagreement** requires either contradictory explicit guidance or materially different solutions to the same explanatory problem. Mere absence is not disagreement.
- A **genre-specific pattern** is concentrated in one or two strata and has a plausible medium or task explanation.
- A **counterexample** is a sampled artifact that omits or reverses a recurring pattern; it constrains claims of universal presence in this corpus. Because effectiveness was not measured, it does not show that the alternative caused or preserved task success.
- Repetition within two units from one source is treated as one source-level signal in cross-source counts.
- The study codes observable choices, not factual correctness, aesthetic quality, reader learning, conversion, popularity, or author intent.

### Sampling changes and rejected artifacts

- **No source substitution was made in any stratum.** All 18 proposed sources and all five required source families remain represented; the final corpus contains 36 analyzed units.
- **Same-source Hello Interview artifact replacement:** the proposed [“Scaling Writes”](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/patterns/scaling-writes) page exposed only its introduction, headings, and a Premium gate. It was rejected under the accessibility rule and replaced with the fully public Core Concepts Quick Reference.
- **High Scalability provenance control:** “Amazon Architecture” is explicitly logged as Todd Hoff's editorial synthesis with a named source list, while “Tagged Architecture” is explicitly logged as a guest insider account. They are not pooled as one authorial voice or treated as Amazon/Tagged-independent validation.
- **No artifact replacement in strata 1, 3, 4, or 5.** The proposed units or directly listed representative pages were accessible; interaction-dependent limits are recorded rather than silently reduced to static prose.
- **Same-source *Grokking Algorithms* artifact replacement:** the proposed second-edition Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 liveBook pages returned 403/subscription responses during analysis. To keep the required official source represented without treating a marketing page as an explanation, the study substituted two coherent, publicly rendered openings from Manning's official first-edition liveBook. The [second-edition product page](https://www.manning.com/books/grokking-algorithms-second-edition) remains metadata only, not an analyzed unit.

## Within-stratum analysis

### 1. Practitioner essayists and software-design authors

**Martin Fowler — artifact observations (O).** “Microservices” places a definition promise and compact characterization in the lede, then explicitly says comparison with a monolith is a useful place to begin. The essay spirals from that contrast through component vocabulary, organizational and production cases, diagrams, characteristics, and a qualified reassessment. Terms with overloaded meanings—*service*, the relation to SOA, and component forms—are distinguished rather than silently normalized. Remote-call cost, distributed failure, interface coordination, consistency, operational complexity, and uncertain long-term maturation sit beside the benefits. Its table of contents, sidebars, captions, footnotes, references, and revision list fit a long definition/position essay; it offers cases and a source trail but no learner check ([“Microservices”](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html)).

“Strangler Fig” chooses a different opening: a vacation scene and botanical mechanism precede the software label. The plant remains a running analogy as the note moves into gradual replacement, seams, transitional architecture, and four activities. It makes causality and cost explicit—small isolated replacements can lower introduction risk and return value sooner, while coexistence code and unchanged organizational culture can recreate or add complexity. A short numbered list and further reading are enough navigation for this bounded note; there is again no exercise ([“Strangler Fig”](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html)). Fowler's comment about beginning with a monolith comparison is coded `G` because it explicitly recommends a presentation move; the architecture prescriptions themselves are not writing guidance.

**Julia Evans — artifact observations (O).** “What's a network interface?” announces that it will present observations, experiments, questions, and guesses rather than a crisp definition. It creates a namespace with no interfaces, records a connection failure, adds loopback, records changed behavior, inspects routes and packets, and only then offers a provisional model. Commands and output are load-bearing verification; terminology follows observed state; surprises and possible error are marked in place. The personal, question-led lab-note voice is therefore part of its epistemic contract, not unfinished imitation of a reference page ([network-interface tutorial](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/09/03/network-interfaces/)).

“Some ways to get better at debugging” does the reverse at smaller scale: it announces a five-part scholarly taxonomy, translates the categories into action headings, and then supplies tools and realistic microexamples. Plain verbs precede academic labels, a numbered structure supports scanning, and causal claims about knowledge and experience remain modestly hedged. The post contains no practice task; unit tests and reproductions are examples *inside* its advice, not reader verification ([debugging essay](https://jvns.ca/blog/2022/08/30/a-way-to-categorize-debugging-skills/)).

**Dan Luu — artifact observations (O).** Both empirical essays convert an early suspicion or user-impact scene into a measurement question and put results before extended interpretation. “Computer latency” operationalizes latency endpoints, leads with a cross-era table, and then decomposes input, scheduling, buffering, display, and pixel transition. “How web bloat impacts users with slow devices” defines its metrics—including a modified `LCP*`—before reading large site/device tables, then follows main-thread work to delayed feedback and user/business consequences. Tables, equations, comparison baselines, and appendices carry evidence that a diagram could not replace ([latency essay](https://danluu.com/input-lag/), [web-bloat study](https://danluu.com/slow-device/)).

The same appendices and adjacent qualifications expose endpoints, controls, confounds, small samples, metric-gaming cases, and counterexamples. Verification here means inspectable setup and possible replication, not exercises. The voice is personal, skeptical, parenthetical, and sometimes sardonic; mechanism headings and audit appendices compensate for long, dense narrative.

**Within-stratum inference (I).** The common source-level signal is not a preferred medium. Fowler makes architecture/organization consequences visible through contrasts and cases; Evans reveals local state change through experiments or categorized practice; Luu decomposes performance through measured pipelines. Qualification likewise changes form: design trade-off, explicit uncertainty, or measurement limitation. This stratum supplies a candidate requirement to expose the claim's mechanism, evidence status, and boundary; it does not favor diagrams, runnable commands, or tables indiscriminately.

**Meaningful disagreement and local scope constraint.** Fowler explicitly supplies and motivates an early comparison/definition for microservices, whereas Evans explicitly withholds a crisp network-interface definition and builds a provisional model through experiments. Evans declares an exploratory, provisional route and omits an upfront crisp definition, locally constraining “always define the concept before evidence.” Verification also varies inside the stratum: Evans's networking unit and Luu's empirical essays expose checks; Fowler's decision essays and Evans's debugging list do not.

### 2. System-design educators and publications

**ByteByteGo — artifact observations (O).** “How does Docker work?” offers a one-sentence outcome, three named components, a central infographic, and an ordered `docker run` trace. Arrows and nearby bullets make image movement and container creation causal and retain a text-accessible path. It does not discuss runtime alternatives, security, caching, failure, setup, or expected output ([Docker guide](https://bytebytego.com/guides/how-does-docker-work/)).

“7 Must-Know Strategies to Scale Your Database” is reference-first: seven parallel labels, one-line mechanisms, and mini-schematics appear without a workload or decision path. Unlike a production case, it omits costs, triggers, and failure modes. It also supplies an important negative case: the infographic's Sharding panel contains a page-resource-loading sentence while the HTML describes splitting tables across servers. Repetition across prose and image did not guarantee cross-modal consistency ([database-scaling guide](https://bytebytego.com/guides/7-must-know-strategies-to-scale-your-database/)). Neither unit contains a reader check.

**High Scalability — artifact observations (O).** “Amazon Architecture” starts by declaring its editorial basis and naming information sources, then organizes platform, scale, architecture, and lessons into a dense reference-like bullet inventory. It defines scalability, compresses examples from teams through CAP and deployment, and includes abundant failure/organizational consequences. The source list is load-bearing for provenance, but individual bullets do not map cleanly back to individual sources, and the voice shifts among paraphrase, imperative generalization, and Todd Hoff's evaluation ([Amazon profile](https://highscalability.com/amazon-architecture/)).

“Tagged Architecture” is explicitly a different provenance and narrative: a guest insider account moves through five dated architecture snapshots at increasing scale. Each version pairs pressure, decision, outcome, and new cost; proximate topology diagrams make system evolution visible. The author reports cache effects, capacity, migration outage, connection limits, and management cost but supplies no reproducible method. The timeline is both navigation and causal structure ([Tagged guest case](https://highscalability.com/tagged-architecture-scaling-to-100-million-users-1000-server/)).

**Hello Interview — artifact observations (O).** “Core Concepts” names interview candidates, distinguishes a time-constrained overview from mastery, and repeatedly uses definition→default→contrast/example→trade-off/common mistake→deep-link sections. Terms, endpoint examples, hotspot cases, capacity arithmetic, diagrams, and a table serve decisions. Common mistakes and failures remain unusually visible for an overview: stateful connections, stale caches, stampedes, Redis outage, premature sharding, hotspots, cross-shard transactions, and consistency costs. Its layered route and on-page navigation support both linear study and lookup. It explicitly guides how candidates should present endpoints, capacity math, and shard-key trade-offs; those presentation directions are `G`, while its technical defaults remain source claims ([Core Concepts](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts)).

The Quick Reference removes the narrative and deep-dive path but retains compact paired choices, alt-texted cards, trigger conditions, and selected costs. It has no standalone audience statement or learner check; it depends on the course contract and serves recall ([Quick Reference](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts/quick-reference)).

**Within-stratum inference (I).** All six units expose content early and express causal relations, but depth ranges from one arrow or sentence to a six-year production evolution. Visual responsibility is genre-specific: simultaneous overview at ByteByteGo, topology-over-time at Tagged, and course card plus self-contained prose at Hello Interview. Authorship is also artifact-level: the two High Scalability pages are editorial synthesis and attributed guest testimony, not one domain voice.

### 5. Documentation, style, and pedagogy systems

**Source facts (F).** The two Google units are pre-class course material: “Audience” gives a ten-minute estimate and “Organizing large documents” a twenty-minute estimate; both retain exercises with revealable possible answers. The course introductions identify Technical Writing One as fundamentals training and Technical Writing Two as intermediate training for people who have completed the first course ([Technical Writing One](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one), [Technical Writing Two](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two)). **Google — artifact observations (O).** “Audience” puts its governing model near the top as a compact equation: useful documentation supplies the knowledge and skills a reader needs for a task minus what the reader already knows. It then moves from that abstraction to role/proximity examples, a fictitious audience analysis, task-oriented learning outcomes, failure examples, and exercises. Terms such as *proximity* and *curse of knowledge* are introduced where the distinction becomes useful. Its examples do more than decorate a rule: changing the stated audience changes which unexplained C concepts count as defects. Causal claims are local and qualified—roles are only a first approximation; knowledge diverges with project and time; idioms and cultural references can obstruct international readers and translation. The page uses direct second person, short headed sections, an estimated time, a next-unit link, and answer-reveal exercises ([Audience](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)).

“Organizing large documents” opens with the reader's organizing problem and a four-item tactic list. It alternates rules with reader personas, sample outlines, rewritten introductions, and exercises. Its own structure demonstrates the navigation it recommends: descriptive and task-oriented headings, orientation text under headings, links, and progressively disclosed sections. Unlike “Audience,” its principal object is not sentence clarity but document topology. Trade-offs are explicit: short documents may better serve a beginner or quick overview; long tutorials and references can support narrative or lookup; a set of interconnected pages can serve readers who search. The unit also proposes document QA by checking the finished draft against its introductory promise ([Organizing large documents](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/large-docs)).

**Google — explicit guidance (G).** Define the reader by role and proximity, list what that reader must learn or do, fit vocabulary to that knowledge, and avoid assuming an expert's context. For large documents, state coverage, prerequisites, and exclusions in the introduction; introduce information near its point of use; alternate concepts with application where useful; begin with simpler examples; and supply navigation. These are source prescriptions, not empirical findings about learning ([Audience](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience), [Organizing large documents](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/large-docs)).

**Diátaxis — artifact observations (O).** “Start here” makes the reading contract unusually explicit: use the site as a toolbox, read the short primer, and follow deeper links only when a work problem calls for them. The four content types and their distinct user needs are stated before examples; a map, a two-axis compass table, short analogies, and a four-step workflow then re-express the classification. Vocabulary stays stable across *tutorial*, *how-to*, *reference*, and *explanation*, and the contrasts carry more explanatory weight than any isolated definition. The page recommends pragmatic application and repetition but supplies no active check, expected outcome, or scored exercise ([Start here](https://diataxis.fr/start-here/)).

“Explanation” likewise begins with a definition and purpose, but its sequence is deliberately discursive: boundaries, value, writing moves, sentence stems, and finally an extended food-and-cooking analogy. It treats causal context, history, technical constraints, choices, counterexamples, and perspective as legitimate explanatory material. Its tone is personal and philosophical, with etymology and metaphor as conceptual tools. A conceptual figure and anchored contents support navigation, but there is no code, formalism, or learner check ([Explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)).

**Diátaxis — explicit guidance (G).** Match a document's form to whether the reader is studying or working and whether the need is action or cognition. Keep explanation bounded and separate from instruction and reference; make connections, supply context and reasons, and admit perspective and alternatives. Diátaxis explicitly warns that explanation may need to approach a subject from several directions, and that overloading a tutorial with it can obstruct the learner's practical encounter ([Start here](https://diataxis.fr/start-here/), [Explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)).

**Microsoft — artifact observations (O).** The quick start is a lookup artifact rather than a lesson: a purpose sentence is followed immediately by short rules grouped under voice, localization, and style headings. Its imperatives enact much of the recommended directness. Examples are deliberately tiny—approved wording, screenshot conditions, and how to position qualifiers—because the unit's job is rapid contributor alignment, not concept construction. The medium supplies table-of-contents and feedback chrome; those platform elements were not coded as authored explanation ([Microsoft Learn style and voice quick start](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/contribute/content/style-quick-start)).

“Code examples” opens by classifying examples by extent and by the developer job they support. It then separates planning from writing. The guide contains almost no sample program itself; its technical contribution is a constraint set for examples: meaningful scenarios, explicit requirements and dependencies, copy/run affordances, expected output, secure defaults, accessibility, intrinsic exception handling, and compilation/testing. The absent worked example is therefore a local exception to any demand that a compact normative reference must itself teach through a running case ([Code examples](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/developer-content/code-examples)).

**Microsoft — explicit guidance (G).** Determine the particular customer and task; put the most important material first; use everyday but still technical language, concise and consistent sentence construction, manageable sections, and an empathetic tone. For code, start with common simple scenarios before greater complexity, explain prerequisites, make the example runnable, show expected output, and test it. The quick start also supplies a medium-specific reason for consistency: localization and machine translation ([quick start](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/contribute/content/style-quick-start), [code examples](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/developer-content/code-examples)).

**Within-stratum inference (I).** All three systems begin by classifying a reader need, but they optimize different levels. Google teaches a writer how to reason and practice; Microsoft compresses organization policy for fast retrieval; Diátaxis classifies whole documentation functions. Their overlap supplies a local candidate to declare the reader, task, scope, and prerequisites. It does **not** supply one stratum-wide document sequence. Google's progressive disclosure and Microsoft's importance-first quick reference address different use conditions from Diátaxis's reflective explanation.

**Meaningful disagreement and local scope constraints.** Google recommends introducing information at the moment it becomes relevant and alternating concept with application; Microsoft tells procedure writers not to interrupt steps with commentary; Diátaxis goes further and places substantial explanation in a separate content type. These are competing answers to *where context belongs*, conditional on genre. Diátaxis's permission for circular, perspective-bearing explanation locally constrains a blanket “always linear and task-first” rule. Microsoft's code-example page likewise constrains “always include a worked example” inside a compact normative reference, even while explicitly requiring examples in the developer documentation it governs.

## Synthesis: recurrence, disagreement, and scope

### Recurring patterns that meet the frozen threshold

Counts below are source-level: a source contributes once when the pattern appears in at least one of its two sampled units. They describe this 18-source corpus, not technical writing in general.

1. **Orient the reader early, but do not mandate one opening form (`P1`: 18 sources, six strata).** Every source puts a task, outcome, question, scope, pressure, definition, or map near the opening. Fowler can begin with a definition promise or botanical scene; Evans can promise experiments instead of certainty; Cloudflare can begin with an operational pressure; Google can begin with a rule. The recurring feature is early orientation, not “always state the thesis in sentence one” ([Fowler's Microservices](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html), [Evans's network-interface exploration](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/09/03/network-interfaces/), [Cloudflare's attack architecture](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-cloudflares-architecture-allows-us-to-scale-to-stop-the-largest-attacks/), [Google's Audience unit](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)).

2. **Build a deliberate progression and often revisit a model (`P2`: 18 sources, six strata).** The sampled artifacts use contrast, phases, generations, cumulative code, progressively repaired models, or overview→detail layering. This is broader than “concrete first”: VisuAlgo begins formally, Ciechanowski builds upward from familiar scenes, GitHub compares system generations, and Nystrom deliberately alternates a map-first chapter with an incremental implementation chapter ([VisuAlgo BST/AVL](https://visualgo.net/en/bst), [Ciechanowski's GPS](https://ciechanow.ski/gps/), [GitHub search history](https://github.blog/engineering/architecture-optimization/a-brief-history-of-code-search-at-github/), [Nystrom's map](https://craftinginterpreters.com/a-map-of-the-territory.html)).

3. **Control terminology at the point where distinctions matter (`P3`: 18 sources, six strata).** Sources define, contrast, translate, or stabilize terms near their first consequential use. Strong cases also admit aliases, overloaded words, disputed names, or model scope instead of presenting one label as frictionless: Fowler distinguishes service/component senses, Red Blob logs coordinate aliases and a notation change, and Kleppmann narrows the formal meanings in CAP ([Microservices](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html), [Hexagonal Grids](https://www.redblobgames.com/grids/hexagons/), [Kleppmann's CAP essay](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)).

4. **Make examples perform a technical function (`P4`: 18 sources, six strata).** The commonality is not realism or length. Examples motivate an abstraction, reveal state, instantiate a rule, test a boundary, falsify a shortcut, justify a production decision, or support transfer. Evans's namespace commands change observable networking state; Red Blob's obstructed map exposes Greedy Best First's failure; Stripe's ambiguous charge tests idempotency; Microsoft's tiny approved/disapproved phrasings serve rule lookup rather than a running lesson ([Evans](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/09/03/network-interfaces/), [A*](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html), [Stripe idempotency](https://stripe.com/blog/idempotency), [Microsoft quick start](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/contribute/content/style-quick-start)).

5. **Choose the non-prose modality by explanatory job (`P5`: 16 observed sources across six strata; one absence; one access-limited).** Tables carry Luu's measurements, command/output blocks test Evans's hypothesis, topology diagrams show Tagged's evolution, code patches expose Nystrom's state, and interactive canvases make Ciechanowski's variables visible. This supports modality–function alignment, not a universal diagram requirement. Microsoft's sampled rules do not use a load-bearing non-prose representation, and the bounded *Grokking Algorithms* renderer did not expose enough image semantics to judge alignment ([Luu's latency study](https://danluu.com/input-lag/), [Tagged architecture](https://highscalability.com/tagged-architecture-scaling-to-100-million-users-1000-server/), [Nystrom's scanner](https://craftinginterpreters.com/scanning.html), [Ciechanowski's Gears](https://ciechanow.ski/gears/)).

6. **Expose cause, transition, or invariant (`P6`: 18 sources, six strata).** Even compact inventories include a condition→effect relation, while deeper artifacts follow a packet, request, pointer, query, signal, migration, or measurement pipeline. The most reusable formulation is: show what changes, why it changes, and what must remain true. Stripe's dual-write case, VisuAlgo's rotations, and GitHub's query lifecycle make this especially explicit ([Stripe migrations](https://stripe.com/blog/online-migrations), [VisuAlgo BST/AVL](https://visualgo.net/en/bst), [GitHub search technology](https://github.blog/engineering/architecture-optimization/the-technology-behind-githubs-new-code-search/)).

7. **Place consequential qualifications near the mechanism (`P7`: 17 sources, six strata).** Trade-offs, failure modes, edge cases, uncertainty, or guarantees appear in every source except ByteByteGo; neither sampled ByteByteGo guide contains the feature. The form is genre-dependent: measurement confounds for Luu, implementation cases for VisuAlgo, model failures for Ciechanowski, organizational costs for High Scalability, and explicit alternatives for Diátaxis. This pattern should be read as “surface the boundary a reader could act on,” not “list every caveat inline” ([Luu's slow-device study](https://danluu.com/slow-device/), [VisuAlgo List](https://visualgo.net/en/list), [Ciechanowski's GPS](https://ciechanow.ski/gps/), [Diátaxis Explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)).

8. **Fit navigation to the reading job (`P8`: 18 sources, six strata).** A long definition essay uses contents, sidebars, and references; an experiment uses question headings; a timeline uses versions; an interactive course uses modes and slide sectors; a reference uses deep anchors; a quick sheet uses parallel cards. The recurrence is retrievable structure proportional to scope, not a mandatory table of contents ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html), [High Scalability's Tagged case](https://highscalability.com/tagged-architecture-scaling-to-100-million-users-1000-server/), [Red Blob's hex-grid reference](https://www.redblobgames.com/grids/hexagons/), [Hello Interview quick reference](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts/quick-reference)).

9. **Include a check, worked outcome, or validation element when the task supports one (`P9`: 10 sources, six strata).** This weaker recurrence spans commands and measurement setup, manipulable predictions, calculations, training, production consistency checks, experiments, expected output, and runnable code. It is not universal: decision essays, visual inventories, retrospective profiles, reflective explanations, and the bounded *Grokking* excerpts omit an active check in the sampled units. Citations and first-party metrics may support an argument, but they are not interchangeable with learner practice or independent reproduction.

Explicit communication guidance (`P10`) appears in only five sources, so it does **not** meet the six-source threshold. Its concentration is itself useful: Google, Diátaxis, and Microsoft are normative systems; Hello Interview teaches answer presentation; Fowler makes one comparison-first recommendation. Most other patterns are analyst observations of artifacts, not rules stated by their authors.

### Meaningful disagreements

- **Where a concept should begin.** Fowler explicitly calls monolith comparison a useful starting point, while Evans explicitly withholds a crisp network-interface definition and builds a provisional model through commands and outcomes. In stratum 3, VisuAlgo is definition-first, Red Blob's A* is demonstration-first, Hexagonal Grids is reference-first, and Ciechanowski is familiar-scene first. These are genuinely different solutions to orientation; the evidence rejects one compulsory concrete/abstract order ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html), [Evans](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/09/03/network-interfaces/), [A*](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html)).

- **Where context belongs relative to action.** Google recommends introducing material near need and alternating concepts with application. Microsoft tells procedure writers to preserve the step flow rather than interrupt it with commentary. Diátaxis treats substantial explanation as a separate content function and explicitly permits a more discursive route. The conflict is resolved by document job, not by declaring one system universally correct ([Google large documents](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/large-docs), [Microsoft quick start](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/contribute/content/style-quick-start), [Diátaxis Explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)).

- **What compactness may omit.** ByteByteGo's database-scaling sheet maximizes a seven-option inventory and omits triggers, costs, and failures. Hello Interview's quick reference uses more text but preserves default conditions, exceptions, and selected failure defenses. Both are lookup artifacts, so length alone cannot explain the difference ([ByteByteGo](https://bytebytego.com/guides/7-must-know-strategies-to-scale-your-database/), [Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts/quick-reference)).

- **How much precision a teaching shorthand carries.** Hello Interview says CAP means that only two of three properties can be had at once, then frames a partition as the practical consistency/availability choice; High Scalability's Amazon synthesis uses closely related “at most two” compression. Kleppmann explicitly rejects classifying whole databases as CP or AP and makes the result depend on formal definitions, operations, configurations, and consistency models. This is a direct terminology/scope disagreement between overview compression and model precision; the present study records it without testing which framing better serves a particular reader ([Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts), [High Scalability](https://highscalability.com/amazon-architecture/), [Kleppmann](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/05/11/please-stop-calling-databases-cp-or-ap.html)).

- **Different confidence mechanisms.** Julia Evans, VisuAlgo, Red Blob, Ciechanowski, Google, and Nystrom provide reader-doable checks or manipulation. Hello Interview and GitHub provide worked outcomes; Luu exposes a potentially reproducible measurement setup; Stripe reports operational validation. Kleppmann's counterexamples/source trail and High Scalability's reported outcomes are argumentative support outside `P9`, not active reader checks. These categories should not be collapsed into a quiz rule.

|---|---|---|

### Counterexamples and negative cases that constrain global rules

- Evans's network-interface unit explicitly declares an exploratory, provisional route and omits an upfront stable definition.
- Luu's tables, Evans's command/output blocks, and Stripe's idempotency prose carry mechanisms without a conventional architecture diagram.
- Fowler's decision essays, ByteByteGo's guides, High Scalability's profiles, and Diátaxis's reflective explanation provide no active learner exercise; exercise presence is not a universal completion test.
- Microsoft's code-example guidance contains almost no worked program itself, although it explicitly requires meaningful, runnable, tested examples in the developer content it governs. A normative reference may prescribe an artifact it does not reproduce in full.
- GitHub's history places dense analyzer detail behind a disclosure while keeping the main limitation visible. “Put every qualification inline” is too strong; optional depth can carry mechanism when the overview preserves the decision-relevant boundary.
- **Negative case:** ByteByteGo's sharding panel conflicts with its adjacent HTML. Repeating information in prose and image is not sufficient; cross-modal consistency requires an explicit check.
- Diátaxis uses a perspective-bearing, sometimes circular route for its stated understanding-oriented job, constraining a blanket rule that all technical material must be linear and task-first.

## Skill implications

These are analyst inferences from the sampled corpus, not claims that the sources proved reader-learning effects. A downstream technical-writing skill can treat them as provisional decision prompts, organized into common and genre-specific branches; their effectiveness should be tested rather than assumed.

### Common decision prompts

1. **Declare the communication contract before drafting.** Capture the intended reader, prior knowledge, task or question, promised outcome, scope, and exclusions. The published opening need not list every field, but it should orient the reader before substantial detail. This operationalizes the strongest overlap among [Google's audience model](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience), [Hello Interview's time/depth contract](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/core-concepts), and the many observed problem-first artifacts.

2. **Choose an explanatory backbone from the job.** Select one primary progression—contrast, trace, phases, successive failures, model construction, map→detail, or reference taxonomy—and state why it fits. Permit reversals: definition-first, concrete-first, and overview-first all have counterexamples in the corpus. For longer work, revisit the same model at higher fidelity rather than introducing unrelated examples at every level.

3. **Maintain a term-and-model ledger.** For each load-bearing term, record its first need, definition or contrast, aliases, notation, and scope. If a term is contested or overloaded, say which model is being used and what the shorthand omits. The CAP disagreement shows why a compressed definition should not silently pose as a source-neutral universal.

4. **Assign every example a technical function.** Require the author to label the example's job internally: motivate, instantiate, trace state, test a rule, expose a boundary, falsify a shortcut, justify a decision, or prompt transfer. Remove decorative examples that do none of these; do not require a full running example in a compact normative reference when paired microexamples better serve lookup.

5. **Make the mechanism inspectable.** Identify the action or condition, the state that changes, the causal link, the consequence, and any invariant or guarantee. Component inventories should include at least one consequential relation. For a production history, carry the same constraint across versions; for an algorithm, carry the same state variables across steps.

6. **Put decision-changing boundaries near the claim.** Prompt for prerequisites, costs, alternatives, failure modes, edge cases, confidence, and recovery. Keep a boundary inline when acting without it would mislead; allow appendices, disclosures, or deep links for audit detail. This preserves the difference between “qualify the claim” and “interrupt every sentence with caveats.”

7. **Choose modality by function and test the handoff.** Use a table for repeated comparison, code/commands for execution, a diagram for topology or spatial relation, animation for temporal transition, and interaction when varying state is itself explanatory. Then check that prose, labels, alt text, and visual state agree. The [ByteByteGo sharding mismatch](https://bytebytego.com/guides/7-must-know-strategies-to-scale-your-database/) should become a specific cross-modal QA test. For interactive work, identify what a static, print, keyboard, or non-pointer reader loses rather than assuming nearby prose is a complete fallback.

8. **Scale navigation to the reading mode.** Ask whether the unit is meant for linear learning, task completion, lookup, audit, or optional depth. Use the smallest adequate combination of descriptive headings, steps, contents, summary, anchors, cards, disclosure, appendices, or cross-links. Do not require a table of contents for a short bounded note or force a reference into a narrative path.

9. **Choose the owed check or argumentative support, not a ritual exercise.** A tutorial may need reader-runnable input and expected output; an interactive explainer a prediction and observable state; an empirical claim methods, controls, and limitations; a migration case reported validation gates and outcomes; a conceptual argument counterexamples and a traceable source trail. Label which elements the reader can perform, which merely expose reasoning, and which are first-party reports that cannot be reproduced from the artifact. A quiz is appropriate only when learner retrieval or transfer is part of the job.

10. **Separate fact, author guidance, observation, and inference during research.** Preserve `F/G/O/I` or an equivalent evidence label in working notes. Record artifact-level byline, date, audience statement, rendering/access limit, and source posture—official guidance, personal essay, editorial synthesis, guest case, or company self-report—before pooling patterns.

### Conditional genre branches

| If the artifact is… | Consider these additions |
|---|---|
| A build tutorial or cumulative chapter | Prerequisites and outcome; incremental runnable state; expected output/error; recovery after failure; exercises or transfer prompts when learning is in scope |
| An interactive visual explanation | A variable→state-change map; controls adjacent to the claim; guided prediction/observation; pause/reset; explicit static and accessibility fallback audit |
| A quick reference or interview overview | Parallel labels; defaults and triggers; at least the decision-changing cost or exception; overview/deep-dive links; no invented narrative if lookup is primary |
| An architecture case or engineering history | Initial constraint/invariant; prior-state failure; decision and transition; reported outcome plus new cost; dated scale; insider/editorial/product provenance |
| An empirical performance essay | Operational definitions; baselines; method and measurement endpoints; tables suited to comparison; confounds, counterexamples, and reproducibility limits |
| A normative style or documentation rule | Rule, rationale, applicable reader/task, positive and negative example where useful, and a compliance check; do not confuse prescription with demonstrated effectiveness |
| A reflective conceptual explanation | Connections, causes, context, alternatives, perspective, and scope; permit a discursive route when understanding—not immediate action—is the job |
| A long book or sequenced course | Chapter-level contract, carried prerequisites, stable recurring notation/examples, map and local route, deliberate recap, and checks consistent with cumulative progress |

### Rules the evidence does not justify

The skill should not hard-code “always begin with a concrete example,” “always include diagrams,” “always add exercises,” “always use a neutral institutional tone,” “always keep every qualification inline,” or “all explanation should be embedded in the procedure.” Each claim has an observed exception in the sampled corpus, and several conflict with explicit genre-conditioned guidance.

## Limitations

- **Purposive, not representative, sampling.** The census intentionally balances recognizable source types; it is not a random sample of technical writing, the web, or even each named author's work. Prominent public English-language sources introduce availability, reputation, survivorship, and language biases.
- **Only two units per source.** Two artifacts cannot characterize a source's complete practice. The units from one source are correlated, which is why recurrence counts each source once. A source-level matrix mark also collapses differences between its two units.
- **Thresholds are decision aids, not statistics.** “Three strata and six sources” was frozen to prevent anecdotal pattern promotion, but it has no inferential confidence interval. Counts indicate coded presence in this corpus, not population prevalence.
- **Single-analyst coding.** The pre-reading codebook reduces post-hoc drift but does not eliminate judgment. There was no second independent coder, adjudication round, or inter-rater reliability estimate. Borderline calls—especially audience inference, modality alignment, and what qualifies as an active check—should be treated cautiously.
- **Presence is not effectiveness.** The study did not observe readers, learning, task completion, retention, accessibility outcomes, persuasion, adoption, or error rates. It cannot show that a recurring feature caused an artifact's reputation or would improve another artifact.
- **No factual-quality audit.** Technical claims, benchmarks, source literature, code, and architectures were not independently validated. The CAP wording disagreement and ByteByteGo image/text mismatch are comparisons of presentation and scope, not a general correctness ranking.
- **Genre, author, topic, medium, age, and editorial setting are confounded.** A pattern concentrated in interactive explainers may arise from interaction, algorithms, audience, author, or all four. Within-source contrasts help but do not identify causal variables.
- **Dynamic and historical artifacts.** Several pages are continuously revised, while architecture and organization posts report 2007–2023 systems and scale. Access-date snapshots may not match earlier or future versions, and reported quantitative outcomes should not be read as current benchmarks.
- **Rendering and access limits.** Static extraction omitted live canvas/SVG state, some styled request/code blocks, Cloudflare slide semantics, and GitHub slideshow frames. Representative controls were checked in one desktop Chromium rendering, not exhaustively across mobile, touch, keyboard, screen readers, bandwidth constraints, or logged-in roles.
- **Bounded official excerpts.** The required *Grokking Algorithms* evidence comes from two coherent first-edition openings because the proposed second-edition chapters were not publicly inspectable.
